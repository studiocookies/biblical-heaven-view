How a 71-meter rocket booster falls back from the edge of space and gets grabbed out of the air by the tower that launched it.

01The problem

Rockets used to be thrown away

For most of spaceflight history, every rocket flew once. The booster, the biggest and most expensive part, burned its fuel, fell into the ocean and was gone.

Picture flying a jetliner across the country and then scrapping it at the gate. That was the rocket business.

The machine costs more than the fuel

Fuel is the cheap part. The engines, tanks and computers are the expensive part. Fly the same booster again and again, and the cost of each launch drops hard.

Falcon 9 proved it. Its boosters land on legs and fly again, some more than 25 times. Super Heavy goes further. It has no legs at all.

02Liftoff and staging

33 engines, all at once

Super Heavy lights 33 Raptor engines and pushes the whole stack off the pad. That's the booster plus Starship, the upper stage, sitting on top. Together they stand about 121 meters tall.

Much of the cloud rolling out from under the mount is steam and water. A water-cooled steel plate under the mount sprays upward to protect the pad.

Max Q

About a minute in, the rocket hits Max Q: the point of peak air pressure on its body. The air is still thick, and the rocket is moving at around the speed of sound.

Hot staging, about 74 km up

Around two and a half minutes in, most of the booster's engines shut down. Starship lights its own engines while it is still sitting on top. A vented ring between the two lets the blast escape.

This is called hot staging. Then the stages separate, and the booster is on its own.

03The turnaround

Flip, then fly home

The booster is still racing away from the coast at thousands of kilometers per hour. Its job is not done.

It flips around until its engines point the way it was going.

The boostback burn

Thirteen engines relight and push against its own speed. This is the boostback burn. It cancels the forward motion and sends the booster arcing back toward the launch site.

Starship keeps climbing on its own engines. You can see it pulling away.

04The fall

Engines off. Fins on.

For a few minutes the booster falls with its engines quiet. It comes down tail first, so the engines face the ground, ready to light.

Steering with grid fins

Four grid fins near the top do the steering. Each one is a big steel lattice that air flows through, like a giant waffle. Tilting them a few degrees is enough to aim a falling 71-meter tube at a single tower.

This scene shows the Flight 5 era booster with four fins. Newer V3 boosters use three larger, fixed fins.

05The landing burn

13 engines, then 3

About a kilometer above the ground, 13 engines light at once: the 3 in the center and the ring of 10 around them. They brake hard.

Then the 10 ring engines shut down. The center 3 do the precise flying: easing the booster sideways toward the tower and slowing it almost to a hover.

Later flights also tested a 13, then 5, then 3 pattern, first flown on Flight 11 over the Gulf of Mexico.

06The catch

Now slow everything down

This is the camera's view from up on the arm. The tower is about 145 meters tall. Near the top, its two arms wait wide open. People call them the chopsticks.

The booster slides in between them on its last 3 engines, slower and slower.

The arms swing closed around it.

The engines cut. The booster settles onto the arms.

About seven minutes after liftoff, a rocket booster is hanging in a tower.

Closed, not clamped

Seen from above, the arms wrap around the hull but don't squeeze it. There is a small gap between them and the steel. The booster hangs from two small points, which the next chapter zooms in on.

Now look at the scale

The booster is 71 meters tall, roughly a 22-story building, and 9 meters wide. Minutes ago it was tens of kilometers up. Now it has stopped in mid-air beside the tower that launched it.

The first time it worked was Flight 5, on October 13, 2024, on the first try. According to later reporting, the attempt came within about a second of being called off.

06bWhere the weight goes

Resting on two hard points

Two short, stout hard points stick out of the booster just below the grid fins. Those rest on rails on top of the arms. The rails carry shock absorbers that soak up the impact.

The tank walls are thin steel. The hard points send the load into strong, reinforced structure instead of the skin.

On V3 boosters, the three larger grid fins themselves become the points the tower lifts and catches.

07The reset

No legs, no barge, no crane

Landing legs are heavy. Every kilogram of landing gear is a kilogram that can't be fuel or cargo. By letting the tower do the landing, Super Heavy carries no legs at all.

And the same arms that caught the booster can lower it straight back onto the launch mount.

The goal: refly in under an hour

When Elon Musk first described the catch in December 2020, he said the idea was to place the booster back on the mount, ready to fly again in under an hour. That is his stated goal. It has not happened yet.

So far, caught boosters have gone back to the factory for inspection and repairs. The first one to fly again was Booster 14. It was caught on Flight 7 and flew again on Flight 9, on May 27, 2025, with 29 of its 33 engines reused.

Next: catch the Ship too

Starship, the upper stage, is meant to come home to a tower as well. That would make the whole rocket reusable. It is harder: the Ship has to survive reentry from orbit first, then flip upright and fly into the arms.

No Ship has been caught yet. Musk has said SpaceX will try to catch both the booster and the Ship on Flight 15. As of November 3, 2026, Flight 15 has not flown. Reports target November 2026, with dates differing between sources.
